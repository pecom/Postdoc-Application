\indent \indent Starting as overdensities in the initial density fluctuations and evolving into the largest gravitationally bound structures in the Universe, galaxy clusters are a rich testbed of the cosmos.
Their large masses, self similarity, combination of both luminous and dark matter, and multi-wavelength observables, make them ideal candidates for studying cosmology. 
My overall goal is to study the growth of structure in the Universe, making galaxy clusters a strong choice as they can give constraints on $\sigma_8(z)$, $\Omega_M(z)$, and $f_{\textrm{gas}}(z)$.

% My overall goal is to study galaxy clusters via weak lensing and constrain the redshift evolution of cosmological parameters and to accomplish that there are a number of important intermediate steps that must be performed.
In order to set competitive limits, a number of systematics must be studied and ideally mitigated, like those induced by optical cluster selection, shear calibration from blending, and model misspecification.
Over the next few years, I plan on both performing early science measurements on clusters from Roman, Rubin, and Euclid along with working on novel methods of mitigation that can be implemented in future data releases.
My previous experiences with cluster, shear calibration, and on-going membership of LSST DESC and Rubin commissioning team equip me with the necessary tools to successfully carry out this research.

\textbf{Previous and Current Research}
% I have spent most of PhD working on weak lensing systematics and validating weak lensing measurements with the Vera Rubin Observatory through cluster studies.
\textit{Blending} During my PhD, I focused on the weak lensing systematic of \textbf{unrecognized blends} which are objects (typically galaxies) that are mistakenly identified as a single entity due to severe amounts of overlap between sources (see Fig.~\ref{fig:blend_ex} for an example).
The repercussions of unrecognized blends via detection bias alone have shown shifts in cosmology parameters at the level of a few percent \cite{ben_clustering, blends_2011, kidsblends_2025}, and an early study including the impact on shape and flux measurements on cosmic shear found up to a 2$\sigma$ shift in S$_8$ \cite{erfan}.

For 3x2pt cosmological analysis, expensive image simulation is used to measure the bias induced by unrecognized blends on redshift and shear calibration.
% Cluster weak lensing suffers from larger shears and more blending, limiting the applicability of this calibration technique. 
Directly identifying unrecognized blends at the catalog level is an appealing solution that I pursued during my PhD.

In \cite{unrec_liang}, we showed that unrecognized blends can be readily detected at the catalog level using machine learning techniques that assign an unrecognized blend probability based on measured fluxes.
While we showed that \textbf{it is possible to remove unrecognized blends from the catalog using observational data alone}, it was unclear if such a removal would ultimately improve our cosmology. 
I investigated this by studying the impact of our blend removal method on shear calibration using the AnaCal shear framework.
We showed that not only does the bias reduce, \textbf{the bias can be almost totally removed} with a 30-40\% sample cut \cite{unrec_shear}.
This is a promising alternative mitigation strategy that can totally remove a large systematic bias that LSST will suffer from.

\textit{Goal 1: Cluster measurements for Roman and Rubin}

%Clusters are an ideal testing ground for shear and photometric redshifts as they tend to have clear signals that can be measured even with only one cluster for both shear and photo-zs.
Precise ensemble measurements of galaxy clusters require an estimate of both statistical and systematic bias.
For clusters, this is traditionally done with N-body or hydrodynamic simulations that can capture physics-induced bias (like miscentering of optical clusters due to triaxiality), but is computationally expensive and can ignore biases coming from image processing.
I propose to work on both direct image generation and synthetic source injection (SSI) to validate shear and redshift measurements coming from science pipelines.

Direct image generation has been the standard technique to calibrate any biases in cosmic shear [macrann, hsc] as it is able to directly test for systematic biases inherent in the tools and algorithms being used.
There are a number of improvements we need to make to these image simulations to properly test clusters, including varying background redshifts, introducing cluster members, intra-cluster light, and cluster triaxiality.
Introducing these systematics into our simulations sequentially will allow us to understand the bias introduced by each feature to inform future work on mitigation strategites.

While image generation is a powerful tool, it relies on correctly understanding and implementing all the systematics that exist in data.
Alternatively, SSI allows us to characterize the existing data and test the measurement pipeline directly on synthetic sources.
In previous works, SSI has been used to validate flux measurements and star-galaxy separation [add citations] and when studying Abell 360, this technique was used to understand the effect of various cuts on the source sample.
Repeating this work for Roman and Rubin clusters on a larger scale will allow us to validate measurements made on the real data.

% Both image generation and SSI are image level techniques that are usually ran on the CPU and can be expensive when ran in the large amounts needed for precision calibration.
% A straight-forward speed up would be to convert these codes to make use of GPUs via JAX-GalSim.
% Being able to use both techniques at scale would allow us to validate both source measurement and cluster mass extraction in Roman and Rubin.
Performing both image simulation and SSI gives us an understanding of what causes biases and the total level of bias present in the data.
Improving on the existing image simulation tools and developing the pipelines to measure biases via SSI are necessary steps for future data releases with more stringent systematics requirements.
My experiences with image simulation to validate blend removal and Abell 360 cluster measurements make cluster validation a natural next step that I am qualified to work on and lead.

With a validated pipeline in hand, we will have confident (or at least properly weighted) cluster weak lensing masses.
For Rubin Data Preview 2 there are $\sim 100$ X-Ray detected clusters by eRosita and $\sim 400$ SZ detected clusters by ACT, which are more than enough to produce a LSST mass calibration for those external samples.
The previous ACT calibration uses a similar number of clusters [citation], so it is possible to of produce competitive results using this early data alone.
Producing cosmology contours from calibration constitutes another project that would require estimating a covariance, modeling shear profiles (either hierarchically or stacked), redshift distribution estimation, and running cosmology chains, all of which are quite new to me.
I am excited to learn and build towards a tomographic understanding of the cosmos.

\textit{Goal 2: Mitigate weak lensing systematics} 

I will continue to work on mitigating weak lensing systematics, especially blending.
% I will continue to work on mitigating weak lensing systematics, especially blending but I am also interested in joint processing with Roman, Rubin, and Euclid.
Extending my previous work on blend removal in \cite{unrec_shear} to include redshift is the next natural step towards understanding the effects of this methodology on cosmology.
% Validating the use of catalog level machine learning to detect unrecognized blends on the intermediate steps like redshift and shear calibration are vital in order to best understand the effect of unrecognized blends on cosmology.
Completing a redshift study would enable a re-analysis of previous data, like DES Y3 or HSC Y3, providing a comprehensive look at the effect of unrecognized blends in weak lensing.

While my previous work on blending has focused on removing these undetected objects, it may be possible to model the effects of blending for better mitigation without incurring a statistical cost.
Using the machine learning probability from \cite{unrec_liang} as a prior, we can treat certain objects as a superposition of two galaxies which can lead to more accurate posteriors for redshift and shear.

For redshift distributions, the self-organizing map (SOM) which maps measured fluxes to a 2D surface, is an effective tool for estimating ensemble distributions $n(z)$ but can also be extended to account for blends as outlined in Fig~\ref{fig:som_ex}.
In that example, we use the blending probability $\lambda$ to combine two redshift probability distributions: the distribution assuming a single galaxy and the distribution assuming a superposition of two galaxies.
In preliminary work, I have shown that this schema can produce more accurate posterior estimates than a single-galaxy SOM approach.
For shears, accounting for cross-redshift blends can be done at the image processing level by improving deblending which can drive down shape noise.
Both of these are relatively straight-forward to show as proof of concept and promising ideas that can improve the quality of data used in cosmological analysis.

The best solution to unrecognized blends would be to directly detect them which can be done through joint processing of space and ground based data.
While there are strong solutions on how to best combine photometry, there is work to be done on combining shape measurements in order to maximize weak lensing signal.
During my postdoc, I plan to contribute to the on-going efforts to enable joint-processing for both Metadetect and AnaCal.
This will mostly rely on simulated datasets, like the existing Roman-Rubin simulations, but can be tested on data using deep field overlap of clusters fields which I am well equipped to study.
